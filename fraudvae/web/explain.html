How it works

FinFraud is two models that look at every payment. One learnt what ordinary payments look like - since the fifth version, with the customer's own previous payments in view - and flags whatever it cannot explain. The other learnt from the labelled fraud what known fraud looks like. A small third piece weighs the two, plus a few extra models, into one probability per bank. This page explains the data, the models, and how one new payment becomes a verdict. Each section starts in plain terms; the technical detail follows under "in detail" for anyone who wants it. Any term you don't know explains itself: rest the pointer on it (or tap it on a phone).

1. The training data

The model was trained on … payments from … public datasets, of which … (…) are labelled fraud. Two are real, anonymised bank data (ULB and Vesta/IEEE-CIS); the rest are simulations calibrated on real behaviour (PaySim, BankSim, Sparkov, the Fraud Detection Handbook simulator, IBM TabFormer, IBM AMLworld), synthetic data released for research (Bank Account Fraud, Capital One) or a teaching dataset (fraud-ecommerce). The mix is deliberate: no single public dataset is both large and rich in detail, and a real bank's data looks like none of them exactly. AMLworld is the odd one out on purpose: account-to-account transfers whose label is money laundering rather than card fraud, so that the model's "unknown bank" mode has seen more than one kind of institution.

Each dataset is split by time: the oldest 70% trains the model, the next 10% tunes it (the validation split), and the newest 20% is only ever used to report numbers (the test split). The Training data page has each dataset's distributions, licence and checksums.

2. One set of fields for eleven datasets

Every dataset is translated into the same 95 numeric and 24 categorical fields. Some exist everywhere (amount, hour, day of week); some only in a few datasets (account balances in PaySim, the CVV check in Capital One, a credit score in TabFormer, distance to the merchant in Sparkov). Merchant categories from different datasets are mapped onto one list of 18, so a grocery purchase is grocery whichever dataset it came from.

The largest group of fields is context: not the payment itself but what surrounds it, computed from each dataset's own timeline. For the customer: payments and spend in the last hour, 24 hours and 7 days; their average amount over 30 days and how far this amount is from it; how many payments we have seen from them before and for how long; seconds since their previous payment; and whether this is their first time at this merchant, in this category, or abroad. For the merchant: its volume in the last 24 hours and this amount compared with what it usually sees. These are the things a fraud analyst looks at first, and they carry more signal than the payment itself: fraud is rarely a strange payment, it is a strange payment for this customer.

Nothing is guessed. A field a dataset cannot provide is marked unknown and left out of the model's calculation. Treating "unknown" as zero would teach the model that half the world has a credit limit of zero.
in detail

The history features are strictly causal: a row only ever sees payments that happened before it. This matters more than it sounds; a window that included the row itself would leak the fraud amount into the "customer's typical amount" and the model would look far better than it is. The first payment we ever see from a customer is "new" on every flag; the prior-count field lets the models discount that.

3. The customer's history and the things around a payment

The fifth version added four things, all about who and what rather than how much.

First, every dataset names its entities where it can: the customer, the counterparty (merchant, terminal or receiving account), the card, the device, the e-mail domain and the billing address. Second, a block of facts about each of them: how many payments this device or address produced in the last 30 days, how many different customers it was seen with for the first time in those 30 days (one device behind forty new cards is the shape of an account-takeover ring), how old it is in the data, and how much confirmed fraud sits in its past. That last one is the feature a careless pipeline gets wrong: a fraud label is not known on the day of the payment, so the model only counts labels old enough to have been known at the time - 30 days in one block, 7 days in another, and the score form asks which your bank can honestly use.

Third, a block of device fields (operating system, browser, screen size, session length) for the datasets that have them. Fourth, each dataset's own raw columns, kept in a side table that only the booster reads (IEEE-CIS has 431 of them). The shared model stays a model of the common language; the booster gets everything the dataset knows. On IEEE-CIS the raw columns and the entity facts together lift the result from 0.86 to 0.94 AUROC and the fraud caught at a 1% false-positive budget from 35% to 56%.

The deepest change is to the model of normal itself. Instead of only the hand-made summary of the customer's past (the counts above, which stay), it now reads the customer's previous payments directly - up to 32 of them - through a small transformer, and both halves of the model see that summary. "Unusual" therefore means unusual given what this customer has been doing, not unusual for the bank. A customer with no history, or a request that sends none, is still scored properly: the model was trained to handle that case.

Three more models arrived with this version, each with its own section on the receipt: a specialist booster per bank with enough labelled fraud of its own; an entity graph per bank, a graph neural network over customers, merchants, devices, e-mails, addresses and cards joined by payments, which is how an address shared by three confirmed frauds speaks even when nothing on the payment itself looks wrong; and two external models that only act when a request carries their input - text models trained on public datasets of malicious links, scam postings and fake profiles, and behaviour models trained on public datasets of mouse, keystroke, touch and motion data that compare a session with the account holder's usual way of using a device.

in detail

The entity block has 29 features: windowed counts (last 30 days), distinct-new-customer counts, ages capped at a year, and delayed-label fraud counts and shares per entity kind. Everything is windowed or capped on purpose: lifetime counts and ages grow with the calendar, and on the thirty-year TabFormer file the booster learnt the era from them and lost a third of its recall on the newest months until they went. The 30-day-delayed pair is part of the schema and seen by both models; the 7-day pair rides in the dataset's wide block for the booster only, with the same counts over the last 7 and 30 days before the label horizon, and the booster is trained with that block blanked on 30% of its rows so a bank whose labels take a month gets a regime the model has seen. The handbook simulator compromises a terminal for 28 days; a month-old label never catches it and a week-old one catches it for three weeks, which is why both delays exist.

The history encoder takes the same field encoding the VAE uses for the current row for each of the last \(L\) rows of that customer (16 on the laptop configuration, 32 on the GPU one), plus the log of the time gap to each, and reads out one vector \(h\). The encoder becomes \(q(z \mid x, h, s)\) and the decoder \(p(x \mid z, h, s)\). During training \(h\) is dropped for a random 10% of rows and replaced by a learned "no history" token. A customer's rows are found through a sequence index built once over the whole prepared set, which is what makes the lookups cheap enough to train on 29 million rows. \(h\) is learnt without labels; the one place it meets them is the sequence head, a small classifier on \([h, \text{embedding of } x]\) trained on the same labelled subsample as the booster, whose logit is one more signal in the blend.

The entity graph is a two-layer GraphSAGE learning a 32-dimensional embedding per node from three time snapshots (fit on the first four fifths of the training period, checked on the last fifth, so a row never sees its own label through its own entities), with a head that reads a payment's entities' embeddings into one signal. The text and behaviour models return calibrated likelihood ratios that are added to the blend's log-odds with a fixed weight; without those inputs the verdict is exactly the blend.

4. The model of "normal": a VAE

A variational autoencoder learns to compress a payment into a short list of numbers (its latent summary) and rebuild the payment from that summary. Trained only on legitimate payments, it gets good at rebuilding what normal traffic looks like. When it rebuilds a payment badly - the amount comes out far from the real one, the hour looks wrong for this customer - the payment is unlike what it knows, and that is the novelty score. Because it never sees a fraud label, it cannot be biased towards the fraud of the past; it only knows normal. One network serves every bank: it is told which dataset a payment came from, so a $40 grocery swipe can be ordinary for one bank and a large cash-out ordinary for another.

in detail

A payment is a vector \(x\). The VAE assumes it was produced by a small hidden vector \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) (\(d\) is 16 to 24 depending on the configuration) drawn from a standard normal prior, then decoded:

$$ z \sim p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad x \sim p_\theta(x \mid z). $$

The decoder \(p_\theta\) is a neural network. Fitting it directly would mean maximising \(\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\), which is intractable, so a second network, the encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2(x))\), approximates the posterior and a lower bound is maximised instead:

$$ \log p_\theta(x) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}} \;=\; \mathcal{L}(x;\theta,\phi). $$

The first term says: encode \(x\) to \(z\), decode it back, and the result should put high probability on the original. The second keeps the codes close to the prior so that the latent space stays smooth and no single payment gets its own private corner. Gradients flow through the sampling step with the reparameterisation \(z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), and the KL between two diagonal Gaussians has a closed form:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \tfrac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\big). $$

5. Numbers, categories and unknowns

A payment is not a picture: the amount is a number with a long tail, the channel is one of five labels, the hour is one of 24. So the model rebuilds each field in its own way - a guess with a margin of error for numbers, a set of probabilities for categories - and a field's "surprise" is measured against the margin the model itself gave. It can be sure about a customer's usual day of the week and vague about their amount, and is penalised accordingly. Unknown fields are skipped: a PaySim payment is never penalised for not having a merchant category.

in detail

The decoder has one output head per field and the reconstruction term is a sum of per-field log-likelihoods, each weighted by its mask bit \(m_f \in \{0,1\}\):

$$ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \;=\; \sum_{f \in \text{numeric}} m_f \log \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_f;\, \mu_f(z),\, \sigma_f^2(z)\big) \;+\; \sum_{g \in \text{categorical}} m_g \log \operatorname{softmax}\big(\ell_g(z)\big)_{x_g}. $$

Numerics are transformed first (\(\log(1+x)\) for amounts, counts and balances, a signed version for things that can be negative) and standardised on the training set. The decoder predicts a variance per field per row. This is the heterogeneous-likelihood idea from HI-VAE (Nazábal et al., 2020) applied to payments. The mask does two jobs: in the encoder it is an input, so "credit limit unknown" is a fact the model can use; in the loss it removes the field.

6. Training on legitimate payments only

The model of normal never sees a fraud row while training. It learns from the legitimate payments of the training split, in several passes (epochs), with a few standard refinements: the pull towards a simple summary is switched on gradually so the model first learns to use the summary at all; one time in ten it is not told which bank a payment came from, so it also learns a bank-agnostic mode for payments from an unknown bank; and the version kept is the pass that did best on the validation split. It is a small network (half a million to three million parameters); the expensive part is the data - about 30 million legitimate rows per pass, minutes on a rented GPU and days on a laptop.

in detail
  • β warm-up. The KL term is scaled by \(\beta\), ramped linearly from 0 to 1 over the first epochs, so the decoder learns to use the latent code before the regulariser pulls it towards the prior (otherwise the optimiser finds the trivial solution of ignoring \(z\)).
  • Source dropout. With probability 0.1 the bank token is replaced by a "generic" token, which is what the scorer uses when a payment's bank is unknown.
  • Checkpoint selection. The v3 and v4 runs kept the epoch with the best validation AUROC (the one place labels touch training: they pick an epoch, not the weights). The v5 history model selects on the held-out ELBO instead, because the per-epoch AUROC estimate swings too much on small fraud sets to stop on.

Architecture: a 3-layer MLP encoder (768-768-384 on the GPU configuration, LayerNorm, SiLU) into a 24-d Gaussian, the mirror image as decoder, plus the history transformer (d_model 128, 2 layers, 4 heads) in v5; 0.64M parameters on the laptop configuration, 2.2M for v4, 3.0M for v5.

7. Scoring how unusual a payment is

To score a new payment the model compresses it, rebuilds it several times (16 draws of its summary, averaged), and adds up how badly each known field was rebuilt. Two versions of that total exist: the raw one, and a normalised one that compares each field's surprise with how badly that field is usually rebuilt for legitimate payments of the same bank - so that a field the model is always vague about (the hour, typically) does not dominate. Tuning picks whichever of the two separates fraud better on the validation split; the active model uses the normalised score. Each field's surprise, in standard deviations, is what the receipt prints next to the field, together with what the model expected instead - "amount $2,100, expected $61" is the model's guess for this customer.

in detail

For a new payment \(x\) from bank \(s\) the encoder gives \(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\); \(K\) samples are drawn, decoded and averaged. The raw score is the negative ELBO estimate:

$$ \text{raw}(x) \;=\; -\frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log p_\theta\big(x_{\text{obs}} \mid z^{(k)}, s\big) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\,\|\,p(z)\big). $$

The per-field negative log-likelihoods \(n_f(x)\) are compared to their own baselines on legitimate validation traffic of the same bank, \(n_f \sim (\bar n_f^{(s)}, s_f^{(s)})\):

$$ \text{norm}(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{|F_{\text{obs}}|}} \sum_{f \in F_{\text{obs}}} \frac{n_f(x) - \bar n_f^{(s)}}{s_f^{(s)}} \;+\; \frac{\mathrm{KL}(x) - \overline{\mathrm{KL}}^{(s)}}{s^{(s)}_{\mathrm{KL}}}. $$

Each term of that sum is a field's surprise on the receipt. The decoder's output is the model's expectation: the mean of the Gaussian head for numbers (back-transformed into the original units) and the probability assigned to the observed class for categories.

8. The model of known fraud: the booster

A model of normal can only find fraud that looks unusual. Where labels exist, a model that has seen them also finds the fraud that looks ordinary: the stolen card used for one plausible grocery run, the account whose only tell is a CVV mismatch. So the second model is a booster: a few hundred to a thousand small decision trees, each asking yes/no questions about the fields ("more than three payments in the last hour and first time at this merchant?"), each one correcting the mistakes of the ones before, trained on the labelled payments. Unknown fields are passed as missing, which a tree can act on: that a dataset never reports a credit limit is itself usable. The receipt shows the booster's reasoning as pushes: how much each field moved its verdict towards or away from fraud, which add up exactly to its answer. They are the counterpart of the model of normal's surprises: one says "this is unusual", the other says "this is what fraud has looked like".

in detail

LightGBM on the same fields as the VAE plus the bank token, the dataset's wide block and the 7-day fraud-history block:

$$ \ell(x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \eta\, f_t(x), \qquad p_{\text{sup}}(\text{fraud} \mid x) = \sigma\big(\ell(x)\big), $$

with \(T\) chosen by early stopping on the mean of two validation AUROCs, one on the validation rows as they are and one on the same rows with the bank blanked, so the generic mode cannot be traded away for a slightly better fit to the named banks. Legitimate rows are subsampled per bank (200k each) and re-weighted towards the base rate, but by at most 3×: on the full corpus the natural weights run from 1 to 85 between banks, and with those one bank's traffic would define "normal" in the generic mode. A 25% slice of the training rows is duplicated with the bank token blanked, which gives the booster a generic mode just as source dropout does for the VAE. The pushes are SHAP values, the exact decomposition \(\ell(x) = b + \sum_f \phi_f(x)\) of the log-odds into one contribution per field.

9. The blend: one probability per bank

The two models disagree in instructive ways. On a simulator whose fraud breaks the rules of normal, the model of normal is already near perfect and the booster adds little; on Capital One the model of normal sees almost nothing and the booster carries the result; on TabFormer the labels are so sparse (0.1%) that a supervised model partly fits noise. So the weighting is per bank, and it is learnt, not guessed: a small model takes the scores of the booster, the model of normal and the extra v5 models, and combines them into one probability with a separate weighting for each bank, fit on the labelled validation rows. The shared weights say how much to trust each model in general; the per-bank adjustments let a bank with strong labels lean on the booster and a bank with weak labels lean on novelty. A bank with very few labelled frauds (ULB has a few dozen) is scored mostly with the weights every bank shares, so it cannot memorise its handful. This blended probability is what the verdict is read from and what the headline numbers report; the novelty verdict is still printed underneath, because it answers a different question - is this new? rather than is this like known fraud? - and is the only one available for a bank with no labels at all.

in detail

One logistic regression on bounded signals - the booster logit, the VAE score, the KL term and, for a v5 model, the sequence, specialist, device and graph logits, each with a presence bit:

$$ p(\text{fraud} \mid x, s) \;=\; \sigma\Big( (\alpha + \alpha_s)\,\bar\ell(x) \;+\; (\gamma + \gamma_s)\, \bar v(x) \;+\; (\kappa + \kappa_s)\, \bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}(x) \;+\; (\lambda + \lambda_s)\, \bar q(x) \;+\; \beta_s \;+\; b \Big), $$

where \(\bar\ell\) is the booster's logit, \(\bar v\) the VAE score standardised against bank \(s\)'s legitimate traffic, \(\bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}\) the standardised KL term, \(\bar q\) the sequence logit, each clipped to a fixed range (the bar), and \(\alpha_s, \gamma_s, \kappa_s, \beta_s\) per-bank adjustments (an interaction with the bank one-hot). A "generic" set of adjustments is fit on rows scored with the bank blanked. Half of the labelled rows fit the weights, the other half checks them, and the whole validation split is then scored once to set thresholds. Each bank's interaction columns are scaled by \(\sqrt{\text{fraud rows} / 10{,}000}\) before the fit, so a bank with few labelled frauds pays more penalty for leaving the shared weights.

Why so few inputs, and why clipped: an earlier version also fed the blend the per-field surprises. They are unbounded (a card paying 10,000× its usual amount has a surprise in the hundreds), and on the 30-year TabFormer file their far tail drifted between the validation months and the newest test months enough to pull the blend below both of its inputs there. A handful of calibrated, clipped signals with per-bank weights are robust to that; the field surprises stay on the receipt as the explanation of the novelty score.

10. From a probability to a decision

A probability only becomes a decision with a threshold, and the right threshold is a business choice: how many honest customers you are willing to bother. For every bank, tuning takes the legitimate validation payments and finds the probability above which exactly 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 2% or 5% of them would be flagged, and records how much fraud that would have caught. The "alert budget" on the Score tab picks one of these. The percentile on the receipt is the same idea read the other way: where this payment sits among legitimate traffic for that bank. The same is done for the model of normal and for the booster separately, so each block on the receipt can say whether it would have flagged the payment on its own. The numbers on the Model tab are computed with those thresholds frozen before the test split is touched, so the false-positive rate you see there is what you would actually have experienced.

11. What it cannot do

  • Labels decide the ceiling. The booster is as good as the fraud it was shown. A bank with no labels gets the novelty score only; a bank with a new fraud pattern gets it only once the pattern is unusual enough for the model of normal to notice.
  • The simulations have simulated fraud. PaySim or BankSim fraud follows the simulator's rules and is easier than real fraud; AMLworld's laundering legs mostly look ordinary and are hard for every model. Treat per-dataset numbers as evidence about the method, not as a promise.
  • Context matters. The unknown-bank mode works, but a named bank is noticeably better because its idea of normal, its blend weights and its thresholds are all its own. A real deployment would fit a new bank on a few weeks of its traffic before going live.
  • One customer of history, one hop of graph. The history model reads one customer's past and the entity graph looks one step out from each payment. Laundering rings and mule networks are structure several steps wide that this does not yet see; that, and richer device data than the public datasets carry, are the next things to add.
