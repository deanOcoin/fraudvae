How it works

Two models look at every transaction. A variational autoencoder learns what ordinary payments look like - since v5 with the customer's previous transactions in view - and scores anything it cannot explain as novel; a gradient-boosted classifier learns from the labelled fraud what known fraud looks like. A small per-bank blender turns the two, and the v5 signals beside them (the bank's specialist, the sequence, device and entity-graph heads, the text and behaviour modules), into one probability. This page covers the data, the models, and how a single new transaction becomes a verdict.

1. The training data

The corpus is … payment transactions from … public datasets, of which … (…) are labelled fraud. Two of the sources are real anonymised bank data (ULB and Vesta/IEEE-CIS); the rest are simulations calibrated on real behaviour (PaySim, BankSim, Sparkov, the Fraud Detection Handbook simulator, IBM TabFormer, IBM AMLworld) or synthetic recruiting data (Capital One). The mix is deliberate: no single public dataset is both large and semantically rich, and a bank's own data looks like none of them exactly. AMLworld is the odd one out on purpose: account-to-account transfers whose label is money laundering rather than card fraud, there so the "generic bank" mode has seen more than one kind of institution.

Each source is split by time: the oldest 70% trains the model, the next 10% calibrates it, the newest 20% is only ever used to report numbers. The training data page has per-source distributions, licenses and checksums.

2. One schema for eleven datasets

Every source is mapped onto the same 62 numeric and 19 categorical fields. Some are universal (amount, hour, day of week), some only exist in a few sources (balances for PaySim, CVV outcome for Capital One, FICO score for TabFormer, distance to merchant for Sparkov, payment format for AMLworld). Merchant categories are collapsed onto an 18-way taxonomy, so a TabFormer MCC 5411, a Sparkov grocery_pos and a BankSim es_hyper all become grocery.

The largest block describes context, computed from each source's own ordering of events. For the customer: transactions and spend in the last hour, 24 hours and 7 days; the mean amount over 30 days and this amount as a z-score against that history; how many transactions we have seen from them before and for how long; seconds since their previous transaction; and three first-time flags: first time at this merchant, first time in this merchant category, first cross-border transaction. For the merchant (or terminal, or payee account), where the source has an id: its volume in the last 24 hours and this amount relative to everything it has seen so far. These are the fields a fraud analyst looks at first, and in practice they carry more signal than anything on the transaction itself: fraud is rarely a strange payment, it is a strange payment for this customer.

Nothing is imputed. A field a source cannot provide is unobserved, carried as a mask bit, and simply absent from the likelihood. Treating "unknown" as zero would teach the model that half the world has a credit limit of zero.

The history features are strictly causal: a row only ever sees transactions that happened before it. This matters more than it sounds; a window that included the row itself would leak the fraud amount into the "customer's typical amount" and the model would look far better than it is. The first transaction we ever see from a customer is "new" on every flag; the prior-count field lets the models discount that.

3. Entities, devices and the customer's sequence

The v5 schema adds four things to that table, all of them about who and what rather than how much. First, every source names its entities where it can: the customer, the counterparty (merchant, terminal or payee account), the card, the device, the e-mail domain and the billing address, as dense integer keys that never reach a model directly. Second, a graph block of 29 features computed from those keys: how many transactions this device or address or e-mail domain produced in the last 30 days, how many customers it was seen with for the first time in those 30 days (one device behind forty new cards is the shape of an account-takeover ring), how old it is in the data (capped at a year), and how much confirmed fraud sits in its past. The last of these is the one feature a careless pipeline gets wrong: a fraud label is not known on the day of the transaction, so the block only counts labels of rows at least thirty days older than the row it describes. The same count and share at a one-week delay (the Fraud Detection Handbook's assumption about how fast a bank learns of a fraud) go to the booster only, as part of the source's wide block: on the handbook simulator a terminal is compromised for 28 days, which a month-old label never catches and a week-old one catches for three weeks, and which delay is honest depends on the deployment's label latency, so both are there: the booster is trained with the one-week block blanked on a share of its rows, the score form asks how fast your labels arrive and blanks it past a week, and the receipt says whether it was used. It is a hand-made one-hop graph summary, causal and delayed, which is what a feature store at a bank would give a model in production. Everything in it is windowed or capped on purpose: lifetime counts and ages grow with the calendar, and on the thirty-year TabFormer file the booster learned the era from them and lost a third of its recall on the newest months until they went.

Third, a device block: operating system and browser families, screen size, the recipient e-mail bucket. Today only the IEEE-CIS source observes any of it (its identity table, present for about a quarter of the rows); the fields exist so the next sources with device telemetry land in the same columns. Fourth, a per-source wide block: the source's own raw columns, anonymised or not, that no shared schema can name. IEEE-CIS carries 431 of them (the V, C, D, M and id columns). They are stored beside the common table, read only by the booster, and never by the VAE, so the novelty model stays a model of the common language while the classifier gets everything the source knows. On IEEE-CIS the wide block and the graph block together lift held-out AUROC from 0.86 to 0.94 and recall at 1% false positives from 35% to 56%.

The deepest change is to the VAE itself. Instead of a hand-made summary of the customer's past (the counts and z-scores above, which stay), the v5 model reads the customer's previous \(L\) transactions directly. A small transformer, the history encoder, takes the same field encoding the VAE uses for the current row for each of the last \(L=16\) (32 on the GPU config) rows of that customer, plus the log of the gap in time to each, and reads out one vector \(h\). Both halves of the VAE see it: the encoder becomes \(q(z \mid x, h, s)\) and the decoder \(p(x \mid z, h, s)\), so "unusual" now means unusual given what this customer has been doing, not unusual for the bank. During training \(h\) is dropped for a random 10% of rows and replaced by a learned "no history" token, so a first transaction, or a caller that sends none, is still scored properly. A customer's rows are found through a sequence index built once over the whole prepared set, oldest first, which is what makes the lookups cheap enough to train on 29 million rows.

\(h\) is learned without labels (the VAE never sees one). The one place it meets them is the sequence head, a small classifier on \([h, \text{embedding of } x]\) trained on the same labelled subsample as the booster. Its logit is the fourth signal on the receipt and in the blend below ("the sequence says..."), kept separate from the booster so the two remain separable explanations. In the app, a caller can send the customer's recent transactions with the request, or just a customer id: the server remembers what it has scored per customer and rebuilds the window itself.

Three more modules arrived with the rest of v5, each with its own section on the receipt. A bank specialist: one extra booster per bank with enough labelled fraud, trained on that bank's rows alone, so the shared booster's capacity is not the ceiling for a bank with a lot of its own data. An entity graph per bank: customers, counterparties, devices, e-mails, addresses and cards as nodes, transactions as the edges between them, a two-layer GraphSAGE learning a 32-dimensional embedding per node from three time snapshots (the encoder fits on the first four fifths of the training period and is checked on the last fifth, so a row never sees its own label through its own entities), and a head that reads a transaction's entities' embeddings into a "graph" signal, which is how an address shared by three confirmed frauds speaks even when nothing on the row itself looks wrong. And two external modules fused rather than blended, because no transaction in the training data carried their inputs: text heads trained on public labelled text-fraud sets (malicious URLs, scam job postings, bot profiles) that read a url or a memo sent with the transaction, and behaviour verifiers trained on public biometric sets (mouse, keystrokes, touch, device motion) that compare a session's raw events with the account holder's enrolled profile. Each returns a calibrated likelihood ratio that is added to the blend's log-odds with a fixed weight; without those payloads the verdict is exactly the blend.

4. What a VAE is

A transaction is a vector \(x\). A variational autoencoder assumes it was produced by a small hidden vector \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) (\(d\) is 16 to 24 depending on the config) drawn from a standard normal prior, then decoded:

$$ z \sim p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad x \sim p_\theta(x \mid z). $$

The decoder \(p_\theta\) is a neural network. Fitting it directly means maximising \(\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\), which is intractable, so a second network, the encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2(x))\), approximates the posterior and we maximise a lower bound instead:

$$ \log p_\theta(x) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}} \;=\; \mathcal{L}(x;\theta,\phi). $$

The first term says: encode \(x\) to \(z\), decode it back, and the result should put high probability on the original. The second term keeps the codes close to the prior so that the latent space stays smooth and no single transaction gets its own private corner. Gradients flow through the sampling step with the reparameterisation \(z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), and the KL between two diagonal Gaussians has a closed form:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \tfrac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\big). $$

5. Mixed types and missing fields

Tabular data is not an image. Amount is a heavy-tailed positive number, channel is one of five labels, hour is one of 24. The decoder therefore has one output head per field and the reconstruction term is a sum of per-field log-likelihoods, each weighted by its mask bit \(m_f \in \{0,1\}\):

$$ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \;=\; \sum_{f \in \text{numeric}} m_f \log \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_f;\, \mu_f(z),\, \sigma_f^2(z)\big) \;+\; \sum_{g \in \text{categorical}} m_g \log \operatorname{softmax}\big(\ell_g(z)\big)_{x_g}. $$

Numerics are transformed first (\(\log(1+x)\) for amounts, counts and balances, a signed version for things that can be negative) and standardised on the training set. The decoder predicts a variance per field per row, so it can be confident about a customer's day-of-week and vague about their amount, and a surprise is measured against that confidence. This is the heterogeneous-likelihood idea from HI-VAE (Nazábal et al., 2020) applied to payments.

The mask does two jobs. In the encoder it is an input, so "credit limit unknown" is a fact the model can use. In the loss it removes the field, so a PaySim row is never penalised for not having a merchant category.

6. Training on legitimate traffic only

The model never sees a fraud row during training. It maximises the ELBO over the legitimate transactions of the training split with a few standard refinements:

  • β warm-up. The KL term is scaled by \(\beta\), ramped linearly from 0 to 1 over the first epochs, so the decoder learns to use the latent code before the regulariser pulls it towards the prior (otherwise the optimiser finds the trivial solution of ignoring \(z\) entirely).
  • Source dropout. With probability 0.1 the source token is replaced by a "generic" token. The model therefore also learns a bank-agnostic mode, which is what the scorer uses when you don't say where a transaction is from.
  • Model selection on validation AUROC. After each epoch the raw score is computed on a validation subsample and the checkpoint with the best mean per-source AUROC is kept. This is the one place labels touch training; it picks an epoch, not the weights.

Architecturally it is small: a 3-layer MLP encoder (512-512-256 in the base config, LayerNorm, SiLU) into a 20-d Gaussian, the mirror image as decoder, between half a million and 2.3 million parameters depending on the config. The expensive part is the data: ~29M legitimate rows per epoch on the full corpus (about a minute per epoch on an RTX 4090, hours on a laptop), which is why the Compute tab exists.

7. Scoring novelty

For a new transaction \(x\) with source \(s\) the encoder gives \(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\); we draw \(K\) samples (8 by default), decode each, and average. The raw score is the negative ELBO estimate:

$$ \text{raw}(x) \;=\; -\frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log p_\theta\big(x_{\text{obs}} \mid z^{(k)}, s\big) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\,\|\,p(z)\big). $$

A low value means "I have seen this kind of thing many times". The raw score has a weakness: it is dominated by whatever field has the widest likelihood, which for most sources is hour of day. So we also keep the per-field negative log-likelihoods \(n_f(x)\) and compare each to its own baseline on legitimate validation traffic from the same source, \(n_f \sim (\bar n_f^{(s)}, s_f^{(s)})\):

$$ \text{norm}(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{|F_{\text{obs}}|}} \sum_{f \in F_{\text{obs}}} \frac{n_f(x) - \bar n_f^{(s)}}{s_f^{(s)}} \;+\; \frac{\mathrm{KL}(x) - \overline{\mathrm{KL}}^{(s)}}{s^{(s)}_{\mathrm{KL}}}. $$

Each term of that sum is exactly what the receipt on the scoring page prints as a field's surprise: how many standard deviations worse than usual the model predicted that field. Calibration computes both scores on the validation split and the active model uses whichever had the higher AUROC. This is the novelty block on the receipt: it never saw a fraud label, so it finds fraud only in so far as fraud looks unusual.

The decoder's output is also shown as the model's expectation: the mean of the Gaussian head for numbers (back-transformed into the original units) and the probability assigned to the observed class for categories. When the receipt says a $2,100 payment had an expectation of $61, that is \(\mu_{\text{amount}}(z)\) for the \(z\) this transaction encodes to.

8. The supervised booster

A density model can only find fraud that looks unusual. Where labels exist, a classifier that has seen them finds the fraud that looks ordinary too: the stolen card used for one plausible grocery run, the account whose only tell is a CVV mismatch. So the second model is a gradient-boosted decision tree ensemble (LightGBM) trained on the labelled rows of the training split, with the same \(F\) fields as the VAE plus the source token as one more categorical. Each tree is a small set of threshold questions ("is the 1-hour count above 3 and is this the first time at the merchant?"); boosting adds a few hundred of them, each fitting what the previous ones got wrong:

$$ \ell(x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \eta\, f_t(x), \qquad p_{\text{sup}}(\text{fraud} \mid x) = \sigma\big(\ell(x)\big), $$

with \(T\) chosen by early stopping on validation AUROC - the mean of two AUROCs, one on the validation rows as they are and one on the same rows with the source blanked, so the generic mode cannot be traded away for a slightly better fit to the named banks. Unobserved fields are passed as missing, which a tree can route on; that a source never reports a credit limit is itself usable. Legitimate rows are subsampled per source (200k each in the shipped config) and re-weighted towards the base rate, but by at most 3×: on the full corpus the natural weights run from 1 to 85 between banks, and with those one bank's traffic would define "normal" in the generic mode. A 25% slice of the training rows is duplicated with the source token blanked, which gives the booster a generic mode just like the VAE's source dropout does.

The receipt shows the booster's per-feature pushes: SHAP values, the exact decomposition \(\ell(x) = b + \sum_f \phi_f(x)\) of the log-odds into one contribution per field, positive toward fraud and negative toward legitimate. They are the supervised counterpart of the VAE's surprises: one says "this is unusual", the other says "this is what fraud has looked like".

9. The blend: one probability per bank

The two models disagree in instructive ways. On a simulator with structural fraud the VAE is already near perfect and the booster adds little; on Capital One the VAE sees almost nothing and the booster carries the result; on TabFormer the novelty signal is reliable and the labels are so sparse (0.1%) that a supervised model partly fits noise. The right weighting is therefore per bank, and it is fit, not guessed. Calibration takes labelled validation rows and fits one logistic regression on bounded signals - the booster logit, the VAE score, the KL term and, for a v5 model, the sequence head's logit and the other module logits (specialist, device, graph), each with the same shape of term:

$$ p(\text{fraud} \mid x, s) \;=\; \sigma\Big( (\alpha + \alpha_s)\,\bar\ell(x) \;+\; (\gamma + \gamma_s)\, \bar v(x) \;+\; (\kappa + \kappa_s)\, \bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}(x) \;+\; (\lambda + \lambda_s)\, \bar q(x) \;+\; \beta_s \;+\; b \Big), $$

where \(\bar\ell\) is the booster's logit, \(\bar v\) the VAE score standardised against bank \(s\)'s legitimate traffic, \(\bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}\) the standardised KL term, \(\bar q\) the sequence logit (with a presence bit, since not every row has a history), each clipped to a fixed range (the bar), and \(\alpha_s, \gamma_s, \kappa_s, \beta_s\) per-bank adjustments (an interaction with the source one-hot). The shared weights say how much to trust each model in general; the per-bank terms let a bank with strong labels lean on the booster and a bank with weak labels lean on novelty. A "generic" set of adjustments is fit on rows scored with the source token blanked. Half of the labelled rows fit the weights, the other half checks them, and the whole validation split is then scored once to set thresholds. A bank's own adjustments are shrunk toward the shared weights in proportion to how few labelled fraud rows it has (ULB has a few dozen in the fitting half; unshrunk, they were memorised), so a small bank is scored mostly with the weights every bank shares.

Why so few inputs, and why clipped: an earlier version also fed the blender the per-field surprises. They are unbounded (a card paying 10,000× its usual amount has a surprise in the hundreds), and on the 30-year TabFormer file their far tail drifted between the validation months and the newest test months enough to pull the blend below both of its inputs there. A handful of calibrated, clipped signals with per-bank weights are robust to that; the field surprises stay on the receipt, where they belong, as the explanation of the novelty score.

This blended probability is what the app flags on and what the headline AUROC on the model page reports. The novelty verdict is still printed underneath, because it answers a different question (is this new? rather than is this like known fraud?) and is the only one available for a bank with no labels at all.

10. From probability to decision

A probability is only useful relative to a threshold, and the right threshold is a business choice: how many legitimate customers you are willing to bother. For every bank the calibration step takes the legitimate validation rows, finds the \(p(\text{fraud})\) quantile that flags exactly 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 2% or 5% of them, and records what recall and precision that bought on the validation frauds. The "alert budget" selector on the scoring page picks one of these operating points. The percentile shown on the receipt is the same quantile function read the other way: where this transaction sits among legitimate traffic for that bank. The same is done separately for the novelty score and for the booster, so each block on the receipt can say whether it would have flagged on its own.

The held-out test numbers on the model page are computed with those validation thresholds frozen, so the FPR you see there is what you would actually have experienced; the "generic" row is the same test rows scored with the bank token blanked, which is the floor for a bank the model has never seen.

11. What it cannot do

  • Labels decide the ceiling. The booster is as good as the fraud it was shown. A bank with no labels gets the novelty score only; a bank with a new fraud pattern gets it only once the pattern is unusual enough for the VAE to notice.
  • The simulations have simulated fraud. PaySim or BankSim fraud follows the simulator's rules and is easier than real fraud; AMLworld's laundering legs mostly look ordinary and are hard for both models. Treat per-source numbers as evidence about the method, not as a promise.
  • Context matters. The generic mode works, but a named bank is noticeably better because the baselines, the blender's weights and the thresholds are all its own. A real deployment would fit a new bank as a tenth context on a few weeks of its traffic before going live.
  • One hop of graph, one customer of sequence. The history encoder reads one customer's past and the graph block is a one-hop summary of each entity's past. Laundering rings and mule networks are multi-hop structure that a graph neural network over the account graph would see and this does not yet; that, device telemetry beyond what IEEE-CIS carries, text and behavioural biometrics are the next blocks (see docs/ROADMAP_V5.md).
