How it works

FinFraud scores every payment with two main models. The first is a variational autoencoder (VAE), trained only on legitimate payments. It measures how unusual a payment is, and since version 5 it compares the payment with the customer's own earlier payments. The second is a booster: a set of decision trees trained on payments labelled as fraud or legitimate. It measures how closely a payment resembles known fraud. A logistic regression called the blend combines the booster's score with the scores of five smaller models into one fraud score for each bank. Since version 6 the booster also reads the output of a transformer pretrained on 328 million unlabelled payments (in version 7, for two of the eleven banks), and since version 7 the blend no longer uses the VAE's score: the VAE explains the payment on the receipt but has no weight in the verdict. This page covers the data, each model, and the steps that turn one new payment into a verdict. Each section starts with the plain explanation, and the technical detail follows under "in detail". Terms set in slightly bold italics show a definition when you rest the pointer on them or tap them.

1. The training data

The models are trained on … payments from … public datasets, and … of them (…) are labelled fraud. Two of the datasets are real, anonymised bank data: ULB (card payments by European cardholders, collected by Worldline) and IEEE-CIS (card-not-present e-commerce from Vesta). Six are simulations tuned to match real behaviour: PaySim, BankSim, Sparkov, the Fraud Detection Handbook simulator, IBM TabFormer and IBM AMLworld. Two are synthetic data released for research (Feedzai's Bank Account Fraud and Capital One's card authorisations), and one is a teaching dataset (fraud-ecommerce). No single public dataset is both large and detailed, and a real bank's data matches none of them, so the corpus combines all eleven. AMLworld is included for a specific reason: its payments are transfers between bank accounts and its label is money laundering, not card fraud, so the mode for an unknown bank is trained on more than one kind of institution.

Each dataset is split by time. The oldest 70% of its payments trains the models, the next 10% (the validation split) tunes them, and the newest 20% (the test split) is used only to report results. The Training data page shows each dataset's distributions, licence and checksums.

2. One set of fields for eleven datasets

Every dataset is translated into the same 95 numeric and 24 categorical fields. A few fields exist in every dataset (amount, hour, day of week). Others exist in only some of them: account balances in PaySim, the CVV check in Capital One, a credit score in TabFormer, the distance to the merchant in Sparkov. Merchant categories from all the datasets are mapped onto one list of 18, so a grocery purchase is grocery whichever dataset it came from.

The largest group of fields is context: facts about what surrounds the payment, computed from each dataset's own timeline. For the customer, these are the number of payments and the amount spent in the last hour, 24 hours and 7 days; the average amount over the last 30 days and how far this amount is from it; how many earlier payments the data holds for the customer and over how long; the seconds since their previous payment; and whether this is their first payment at this merchant, in this category or abroad. For the merchant, they are its number of payments in the last 24 hours and how this amount compares with its usual amount. These are the first things a fraud analyst checks, and they carry more signal than the payment's own fields, because a fraudulent payment is usually unusual for this customer rather than unusual in general.

Missing information is never filled in. A field that a dataset does not provide is marked unknown and left out of the model's calculation. If unknown fields were set to zero instead, the model would learn that most customers have a credit limit of zero.
in detail

The context features are strictly causal: each row is computed only from payments that happened before it. If a window included the row itself, the fraud amount would leak into the customer's "typical amount" and the test results would look better than they are. The first payment seen from a customer is "new" on every first-time flag, and the field that counts the customer's earlier payments lets the models discount those flags.

3. The customer's history and the things around a payment

Version 5 added four kinds of input. All four describe who and what a payment involves rather than the payment itself.

First, each dataset names its entities where it can: the customer, the counterparty (merchant, terminal or receiving account), the card, the device, the e-mail domain and the billing address. Second, a block of facts describes each entity: how many payments it produced in the last 30 days; how many different customers it was seen with for the first time in those 30 days (one device used with forty new cards is the pattern of an account-takeover ring); how long it has been in the data; and how much confirmed fraud it has had. The last of these needs care. A fraud label is not known on the day of the payment, so the feature counts only the labels that would have been known by then: labels at least 30 days old in one block and at least 7 days old in another. The Score tab asks which of the two matches your bank.

Third, a block of device fields (operating system, browser, screen size, session length) for the datasets that have them. Fourth, each dataset's own raw columns, kept in a separate block that only the booster reads (IEEE-CIS has 431 of them). The shared fields stay common to every dataset, and the booster also gets everything a dataset records. On IEEE-CIS the raw columns and the entity facts together raised the blend's AUROC from 0.86 to 0.94 and the share of fraud caught at a 1% false-positive rate from 35% to 56%.

The largest change in version 5 was to the VAE itself. Besides the summary counts above, which remain, it reads the customer's previous payments directly: up to 32 of them, through a small transformer, and both halves of the VAE receive the result. "Unusual" therefore means unusual given what this customer has been doing, not unusual for the bank. A customer with no history, or a request that sends none, is still scored correctly, because the VAE was trained with the history removed from a random 10% of its rows.

Version 5 also added supporting models, each with its own section on the receipt: a specialist booster for each bank with at least 300 labelled frauds; an entity graph for each bank, a graph neural network over customers, merchants, devices, e-mail domains, addresses and cards, linked by the payments between them, so that an address shared with three confirmed frauds raises the score even when nothing on the payment itself looks wrong; a sequence head and a device head, two small classifiers on the VAE's reading of the history and on the device fields; and two kinds of external model that act only when a request includes their input. The external models are text models trained on public datasets of malicious links, scam job postings and fake profiles, and behaviour models trained on public datasets of mouse, keystroke, touch and motion data, which compare a session with the account holder's usual behaviour.

Versions 6 and 7 added a much larger reader of the same history: a transformer with 128 million parameters. It was trained without fraud labels to predict a customer's next payment, field by field, from the payments before it, on 328 million payments from sixteen datasets. For each new payment it produces two things: a summary of the customer's history up to this payment (768 numbers), and how unexpected each field of this payment is given that history (119 numbers and their mean). Neither is a verdict. Both go to the booster as 888 extra columns. In version 7 they go only to the two banks where they improved the results, TabFormer and the Fraud Detection Handbook data, and a small supervised model trained on these columns adds one more score to the blend. The receipt's history model section shows what it read. The history model needs the customer's earlier payments to arrive with the payment, so the example rows carry their customer's last eight payments. A payment whose own fields count earlier payments, but which arrives without them, is judged by version 7 without the history model.

in detail

The entity block has 29 features: counts over the last 30 days, counts of distinct new customers, ages capped at one year, and delayed-label fraud counts and shares for each kind of entity. Every one of them is windowed or capped on purpose. Lifetime counts and ages grow with the calendar, and on TabFormer, whose data covers thirty years, the booster learnt the era from them and lost a third of its recall on the newest months until they were removed. The 30-day-delayed pair is part of the shared schema, and both models see it. The 7-day pair sits in the dataset's wide block, which only the booster reads, and holds the same counts over the last 7 and 30 days before the label horizon. The booster is trained with that block blanked on 30% of its rows, so a bank whose labels take a month is a case it has seen. The handbook simulator compromises a terminal for 28 days: a label that becomes usable after a month never catches it, and a label usable after a week catches it for three weeks. That is why both delays exist.

The history encoder inside the VAE takes, for each of the customer's last \(L\) rows (16 in the laptop configuration, 32 in the GPU one), the same field encoding that the VAE uses for the current row, plus the log of the time gap to that row, and reads them out into one vector \(h\). The encoder becomes \(q(z \mid x, h, s)\) and the decoder \(p(x \mid z, h, s)\). During training, \(h\) is replaced by a learned "no history" token for a random 10% of rows. A customer's earlier rows are found through a sequence index built once over the whole prepared set, which makes the lookups cheap enough to train on 29 million rows. \(h\) is learnt without labels. It meets labels in one place only: the sequence head, a small classifier on \([h, \text{embedding of } x]\) trained on the same labelled subsample as the booster, whose logit is one more input to the blend.

The entity graph is a two-layer GraphSAGE network that learns a 32-dimensional embedding for each node from three time snapshots. It is fit on the first four fifths of the training period and checked on the last fifth, so a row never sees its own label through its own entities. A head reads the embeddings of a payment's entities and returns one signal. The text and behaviour models return calibrated likelihood ratios, which are added to the blend's log-odds with a fixed weight. Without those inputs the verdict is exactly the blend.

The transaction encoder (v6) turns every field into a token: a numeric field into one of 64 buckets, the gap since the previous payment into one of 40, and a categorical field into its own value. That gives 6,389 tokens over 121 fields, and each source uses only the 18 to 57 fields it fills. Four transformer layers read the fields of one payment, and twelve causal layers of width 768 read up to 128 payments in order. The encoder is trained to predict the next payment's fields, with a masked-field loss beside it. For each row it leaves its state after the customer's previous payments (768 numbers), the negative log-likelihood of each of the row's 119 content fields given that state, and their mean. v6-base-e gives this block to the booster for every source. A control experiment shuffled the block's rows within each source, so that the columns carried no information, and it reproduced v6's losses on Capital One and IEEE: the losses came from adding 888 columns to the trees, not from anything the encoder read. v7-f therefore routes the block only to TabFormer and the handbook data (txfm.sources), adds a supervised head on it (the txsup signal), and fits the blend without the VAE's columns (boost.blend_without_vae). When serving, a row from a bank that the block is not routed to is never encoded, and a routed row whose own fields claim a past but which arrives without it is scored without the block instead of being read as a first payment.

4. The model of normal payments: a VAE

A variational autoencoder (VAE) learns to compress a payment into a short list of numbers, its latent summary (24 numbers in the shipped configuration), and to rebuild the payment from that summary. It is trained only on legitimate payments, so it becomes good at rebuilding normal traffic. When it rebuilds a payment badly, for example when the amount comes out far from the real one or the hour is wrong for this customer, the payment is unlike the payments it learnt from. The size of that error is the novelty score. The VAE never sees a fraud label, so it is not biased towards the fraud of the past; it only knows what normal looks like. One network serves every bank. It is told which dataset a payment came from, so a $40 grocery purchase can be ordinary for one bank and a large cash-out ordinary for another.

in detail

A payment is a vector \(x\). The VAE assumes that \(x\) was produced from a small hidden vector \(z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) (\(d\) is 16 in the laptop configuration and 24 in the GPU one), drawn from a standard normal prior and then decoded:

$$ z \sim p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad x \sim p_\theta(x \mid z). $$

The decoder \(p_\theta\) is a neural network. Fitting it directly would mean maximising \(\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\), which cannot be computed. A second network, the encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2(x))\), therefore approximates the posterior, and a lower bound is maximised instead:

$$ \log p_\theta(x) \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}} \;=\; \mathcal{L}(x;\theta,\phi). $$

The first term is the reconstruction: encode \(x\) to \(z\), decode it, and the result should assign a high probability to the original. The second term keeps the codes close to the prior, so the latent space stays smooth and no single payment gets a region to itself. Gradients pass through the sampling step by the reparameterisation \(z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), and the KL divergence between two diagonal Gaussians has a closed form:

$$ D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \tfrac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log \sigma_j^2 - 1\big). $$

5. Numbers, categories and unknowns

Payment fields are not all of one kind. The amount is a number with a long tail, the channel is one of five labels, and the hour is one of 24. The VAE therefore rebuilds each field in the way that suits its type: a predicted value with its own margin of error for a number, and a probability for each possible value of a category. A field's surprise is measured against the margin the model itself gave. The model can be confident about a customer's usual day of the week and uncertain about their amount, and it is penalised accordingly. Unknown fields are skipped, so a PaySim payment is never penalised for having no merchant category.

in detail

The decoder has one output head per field, and the reconstruction term is a sum of per-field log-likelihoods, each multiplied by the field's mask bit \(m_f \in \{0,1\}\):

$$ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \;=\; \sum_{f \in \text{numeric}} m_f \log \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_f;\, \mu_f(z),\, \sigma_f^2(z)\big) \;+\; \sum_{g \in \text{categorical}} m_g \log \operatorname{softmax}\big(\ell_g(z)\big)_{x_g}. $$

Numeric fields are transformed first (\(\log(1+x)\) for amounts, counts and balances, and a signed version for fields that can be negative) and standardised on the training set. The decoder predicts a variance for each field of each row. This is the heterogeneous-likelihood design of HI-VAE (Nazábal et al., 2020), applied to payments. The mask has two roles. In the encoder it is an input, so "credit limit unknown" is a fact the model can use; in the loss it removes the field.

6. Training on legitimate payments only

The VAE never sees a fraud row during training. It learns from the legitimate payments of the training split over repeated passes (epochs), with three standard techniques. The pull towards a simple summary is switched on gradually over the first five epochs, so the model first learns to use the summary at all. For one payment in ten the model is not told which bank the payment came from, so it also learns a mode for payments from an unknown bank. The version kept is the epoch that did best on the validation split. The network itself is small: 0.64 million parameters in the laptop configuration, 2.2 million in the GPU configuration of versions 3 and 4, and 3.0 million in version 5, whose history encoder accounts for the difference. The cost is in the data. One epoch of version 5 reads 29.9 million legitimate rows and took 5.8 minutes on a rented H200 GPU, so its 30 epochs took 2.9 hours.

in detail
  • β warm-up. The KL term is multiplied by \(\beta\), which rises linearly from 0 to 1 over the first five epochs. The decoder learns to use the latent code before the regulariser pulls it towards the prior; without the warm-up the optimiser finds the trivial solution of ignoring \(z\).
  • Source dropout. With probability 0.1 the bank token is replaced by a "generic" token. The scorer uses that token when a payment's bank is unknown.
  • Checkpoint selection. The v3 and v4 runs kept the epoch with the best validation AUROC. That is the only place labels touch the VAE's training, and they choose an epoch, not the weights. The v5 VAE selects on the held-out ELBO instead, because the per-epoch AUROC estimate varies too much on small fraud sets to stop on.

Architecture: a three-layer MLP encoder (768-768-384 in the GPU configuration, with LayerNorm and SiLU) into a 24-dimensional Gaussian, a mirror-image decoder, and in v5 the history transformer (d_model 128, 2 layers, 4 heads). The parameter counts are 0.64M in the laptop configuration, 2.2M for v4 and 3.0M for v5. Version 5 trains on batches of 8,192 rows with a learning rate of 0.0015 and stops after 8 epochs without improvement on the validation ELBO.

7. Scoring how unusual a payment is

To score a new payment, the VAE compresses it, rebuilds it from 16 samples of its summary, and averages how badly each known field was rebuilt. The total comes in two versions. The raw score adds up the errors. The normalised score first compares each field's error with how badly that field is usually rebuilt for legitimate payments of the same bank, so that a field the model is always uncertain about (usually the hour) does not dominate. Calibration picks whichever version separates fraud better on the validation split, and the loaded model uses the normalised score. The receipt prints each field's surprise, in standard deviations, next to the field, together with what the VAE expected instead. In "amount $2,100, expected $61", $61 is the VAE's estimate for this customer.

in detail

For a new payment \(x\) from bank \(s\), the encoder gives \(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\). \(K = 16\) samples are drawn, decoded and averaged. The raw score is the estimate of the negative ELBO:

$$ \text{raw}(x) \;=\; -\frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log p_\theta\big(x_{\text{obs}} \mid z^{(k)}, s\big) \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x, s)\,\|\,p(z)\big). $$

Each field's negative log-likelihood \(n_f(x)\) is compared with its own baseline on legitimate validation traffic of the same bank, \(n_f \sim (\bar n_f^{(s)}, s_f^{(s)})\):

$$ \text{norm}(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{|F_{\text{obs}}|}} \sum_{f \in F_{\text{obs}}} \frac{n_f(x) - \bar n_f^{(s)}}{s_f^{(s)}} \;+\; \frac{\mathrm{KL}(x) - \overline{\mathrm{KL}}^{(s)}}{s^{(s)}_{\mathrm{KL}}}. $$

Each term of that sum is one field's surprise on the receipt. The decoder's output is the model's expectation: for a number, the mean of the Gaussian head, transformed back into the original units; for a category, the probability given to the observed value.

8. The model of known fraud: the booster

The VAE can only find fraud that looks unusual. A model trained on labels can also find fraud that looks ordinary: a stolen card used once for a normal grocery purchase, or an account whose only sign is a CVV mismatch. The second model is therefore a booster, a sequence of decision trees. Each tree answers yes/no questions about the fields ("more than three payments in the last hour and the first time at this merchant?"), and each tree corrects the errors of the trees before it. The shipped booster has 1,702 trees of up to 127 leaves each, trained on 2.19 million labelled payments, 93,111 of them fraud. Unknown fields reach it as missing values, and a tree can use that fact, since a dataset that never reports a credit limit is itself informative. The receipt shows the booster's reasoning as pushes: how far each field moved its answer towards or away from fraud. The pushes add up exactly to its answer. They are the counterpart of the VAE's surprises: the surprises say what is unusual about the payment, and the pushes say what about it has resembled fraud.

in detail

LightGBM on the same fields as the VAE, plus the bank token, the dataset's wide block, the 7-day fraud-history block and, from v6, the history model's 888 columns:

$$ \ell(x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \eta\, f_t(x), \qquad p_{\text{sup}}(\text{fraud} \mid x) = \sigma\big(\ell(x)\big), $$

with learning rate \(\eta = 0.03\) and \(T\) chosen by early stopping (at most 3,000 rounds, stopping after 200 without improvement) on the mean of two validation AUROCs: one on the validation rows as they are, and one on the same rows with the bank blanked, so the generic mode is not traded away for a slightly better fit to the named banks. Legitimate rows are subsampled to 200,000 per bank and re-weighted towards each bank's base rate, with the weight capped at 3. Without the cap the natural weights run from 1 to 85 between banks, and one bank's traffic would define "normal" in the generic mode. A copy of 25% of the training rows with the bank token blanked gives the booster its generic mode, as source dropout does for the VAE. The pushes are SHAP values: the exact decomposition \(\ell(x) = b + \sum_f \phi_f(x)\) of the log-odds into one contribution per field.

9. The blend: one fraud score per bank

The two main models are strong on different datasets. On version 7's test split, the VAE alone reaches an AUROC of 0.990 on BankSim, whose simulated fraud breaks the patterns of normal traffic, and the booster's 1.000 adds little. On Capital One the VAE reaches 0.613 and the booster 0.837, so the booster carries the result. The right weighting therefore differs from bank to bank, and it is learnt from data rather than set by hand. The blend is a logistic regression that takes the booster's score and the scores of the supporting models and combines them with weights shared across all banks plus an adjustment for each bank, fit on the labelled validation rows. The shared weights say how much to trust each model in general. The per-bank adjustments let a bank with plentiful labels rely more on the booster. A bank with few labelled frauds (ULB has 21 among the rows the blend is fit on) is held close to the shared weights, so it cannot memorise its handful of cases. The blend's output is the fraud score: the number the verdict is read from and the headline results report.

The fraud score runs from 0 to 100, but it is not the real-world chance that a payment is fraud. The regression weights fraud and legitimate rows equally, so a score of 50 does not mean an even chance. Decisions use thresholds measured on legitimate traffic instead, as section 10 describes. The novelty verdict is still printed below the main one, because it answers a different question: is this payment new? rather than does it look like known fraud? For a bank with no labels at all, it is the only verdict available. Since version 7 the blend is fit without the VAE's two inputs. Measured as the share of fraud caught at a 1% false-positive rate, those inputs cost slightly more than they added once the booster read the history model. The novelty verdict keeps its own line on the receipt and can flag a payment on that line, but it no longer moves the main verdict.

in detail

One logistic regression on a small set of bounded signals: the booster's logit, the VAE score and its KL term and, for v5 and later, the sequence, specialist, device and graph logits, each with a presence bit:

$$ p(\text{fraud} \mid x, s) \;=\; \sigma\Big( (\alpha + \alpha_s)\,\bar\ell(x) \;+\; (\gamma + \gamma_s)\, \bar v(x) \;+\; (\kappa + \kappa_s)\, \bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}(x) \;+\; (\lambda + \lambda_s)\, \bar q(x) \;+\; \beta_s \;+\; b \Big), $$

where \(\bar\ell\) is the booster's logit, \(\bar v\) the VAE score standardised against bank \(s\)'s legitimate traffic, \(\bar z_{\mathrm{KL}}\) the standardised KL term and \(\bar q\) the sequence logit, each clipped to a fixed range (the bar), and \(\alpha_s, \gamma_s, \kappa_s, \beta_s\) are the per-bank adjustments, fit as interactions with the bank's one-hot code. A separate "generic" set of adjustments is fit on rows scored with the bank blanked. The weights are fit on 523,978 labelled validation rows and checked on another 523,977; the whole validation split is then scored once to set the thresholds. Each bank's interaction columns are scaled by \(\sqrt{\text{fraud rows} / 10{,}000}\) before the fit, so a bank with few labelled frauds pays a larger penalty for moving away from the shared weights. The regularisation strength is \(C = 0.5\) and the two classes are weighted equally, which is why the output is a score and not a calibrated probability. In v7 the \(\gamma\) and \(\kappa\) terms and their per-bank adjustments are left out, and the history model's supervised logit joins the other signals for the two banks that have it; v7's blend has 144 weights.

Why so few inputs, and why they are clipped: an earlier version also gave the blend the per-field surprises. These are unbounded (a card paying 10,000 times its usual amount has a surprise in the hundreds), and on the 30-year TabFormer file their far tail drifted between the validation months and the newest test months. The drift pulled the blend below both of its inputs on TabFormer. A few calibrated, clipped signals with per-bank weights are robust to that kind of drift, and the field surprises stay on the receipt as the explanation of the novelty score.

10. From a fraud score to a decision

A score becomes a decision only with a threshold, and the right threshold is a business choice: how many honest customers the bank is willing to inconvenience. For each bank, calibration takes the legitimate payments of the validation split and finds the score above which exactly 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 2% or 5% of them would be flagged, and it records how much fraud each threshold would have caught. The alert budget on the Score tab selects one of these thresholds. The percentile on the receipt is the same measurement read the other way: the share of that bank's legitimate payments that score below this one. The same thresholds are computed for the VAE and for the booster separately, so each section of the receipt can say whether that model alone would have flagged the payment. The results on the Model tab use these thresholds as they were frozen before the test split was scored, so the false-positive rates shown there are the rates a bank would actually have seen.

11. What it cannot do

  • Labels set the ceiling. The booster can only learn the fraud it was shown. A bank with no labels gets the novelty score only, and a new fraud pattern is caught only once it is unusual enough for the VAE to notice.
  • The simulations contain simulated fraud. PaySim, BankSim and AMLworld generate their fraud and laundering from fixed rules, which a model can learn, and the shipped blend separates all three at an AUROC of 0.990 or more. Real fraud is not generated from rules. The per-dataset results are evidence about the method, not a promise about a real bank.
  • A named bank scores better than an unknown one. Over all the test rows together, the blend's AUROC is 0.988 when each payment's bank is named and 0.971 when the bank is blanked. A named bank is judged against its own normal traffic, its own blend weights and its own thresholds; an unknown bank gets the generic version of all three. The generic results are therefore what a new bank gets until its own payments have been used to fit its weights and thresholds.
  • The history has to arrive with the payment. The models that read a customer's past were trained and measured with every customer's earlier payments available. To them, a payment sent alone is a customer's first payment: version 6 then raises false alarms on customers with a long history, and version 7 sets its history model aside for that payment. An installation of your own stores each customer's payments for this purpose. This site stores nothing, so the history has to come with the request, as it does with the example rows.
  • One customer's history, one step of the graph. The history model reads one customer's past, and the entity graph looks one step out from each payment. Laundering rings and mule networks are structures several steps wide, and the current models do not see them. Wider graph features, and richer device data than the public datasets contain, are the next additions.
