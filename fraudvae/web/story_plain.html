Development process, in plain language

This is the same account as the full version, written for readers who do not work with machine learning. The few technical terms it needs are explained where they first appear and defined again in the glossary at the end.

1. What you are looking at

FinFraud is a program that examines one payment (its amount, the time of day, how it was paid, where, and which customer made it) and returns a verdict and a fraud score from 0 to 100, together with the reasons for both. It was built in eight versions, and all eight are on this site. On the Score a transaction tab you can choose which version judges a payment, or tick Compare every version to see all eight judge the same payment side by side. The newest version, v7, is also the most accurate; the older ones are kept so you can see what each step changed.

The site does not learn from anything you send, and it does not keep it. Each visitor can send 60 scoring requests and 4 file uploads a minute, so nobody can take over the server. Training happens only in the repository's own app, on your computer or on a machine you rent.

2. The idea

There are two ways to spot a fraudulent payment, and they catch different fraud.

The first is to learn from past fraud. Given many payments that were later confirmed as fraud, a program can learn what they have in common (odd hours, a card used far from home, a rapid series of small purchases) and look for the same pattern in new payments. This is called the supervised approach, because people supervised the learning by labelling the examples. It is very good at catching fraud that resembles fraud already seen, and it cannot recognise a kind of fraud it has never seen.

The second is to learn what normal payments look like and flag anything that does not fit. FinFraud does this with a variational autoencoder, a type of neural network that learns to summarise a payment in a few numbers and to rebuild the payment from that summary. When it rebuilds a payment badly, the payment does not fit what it knows: it is novel, and the program can say exactly which parts did not fit (the amount, the hour, the merchant). This model learns only from legitimate payments and never from a fraud label, so it is not tied to the kinds of fraud seen in the past. Since version 5 it also reads the customer's previous payments first, so "unusual" means unusual for this customer, not unusual in general.

FinFraud keeps both. A third, small model, the blend, decides how much to trust each of them, with separate weights for each bank. The blend's output is the fraud score you see, and every score comes with a receipt that lists what pushed it up or down.

Versions 6 and 7 add one more kind of model: a large model that learnt, from 328 million payments and without any fraud labels, what a customer's next payment usually looks like. It does not judge payments itself. Its reading of the customer's earlier payments is given to the supervised model as 888 extra facts (in version 7, for two of the eleven banks only). In version 7 the blend also stopped using the novelty model: once the supervised model had those facts, including the novelty model made the blend slightly worse at catching fraud, so version 7's blend is built without it. The novelty model still explains every payment on the receipt.

3. The data

Banks do not publish their customers' payments, so FinFraud was trained on eleven public datasets. Two of them are real, anonymised payment data: two days of European card payments from 2013, collected by Worldline and the Université Libre de Bruxelles, and six months of online card payments from the payment company Vesta. The other nine are simulations or synthetic datasets published for fraud and money-laundering research. Together they contain 42.8 million payments, of which 133 thousand (0.31%) are labelled fraud. Each dataset was translated into one common format, the same 119 fields for every dataset (95 numbers and 24 categories), with "unknown" allowed wherever a dataset lacks a field, and each dataset is treated as a separate "bank" that the model knows by name. For each bank, the model learnt from the oldest 70% of its payments, used the next 10% to decide when to stop training and to calibrate its scores, and was tested on the newest 20%, which it never saw during training. Every result on this page comes from that newest 20%. Testing on the newest payments is the fair test, because fraud changes over time and a real model is always judged on payments that come after its training data.

The datasets themselves are not distributed here; each has its own licence. Six of them allow sharing samples, and 300 payments from each of those six (179 from the European card data) are the examples you can pick on the Score tab. All eleven datasets are public: some need a free Kaggle account, one also requires joining a Kaggle competition, and Kaggle refuses some VPN connections. The Training data tab says where each dataset can be downloaded. The repository has a command that downloads them and then checks each file's size and checksum against the files the models were built from, so you know your copy is exactly the data the models learnt from.

4. How it improved, version by version

The table below is generated from the versions on this server, with how long each took to train and on what machine. The text after it explains what each step changed. The main measure is AUROC, a score between 0.5 (a coin flip) and 1.0 (perfect) that measures how well the model ranks fraud above legitimate payments. The "9 shared banks" column averages the same nine datasets for every version, so its numbers can be compared directly.

Version 3, on two processor cores

The first complete model was the novelty model alone. It trained in 32 minutes on two processor cores, without a graphics card, on 3.5 million legitimate payments (at most 500,000 from each of eight datasets). Its average AUROC was 0.829. It did well on the simulated datasets whose fraud makes the payment itself unusual (between 0.94 and 0.98) and badly on the two whose fraud looks like an ordinary purchase (0.52 and 0.66; 0.52 is barely better than a coin flip). This is the known weakness of looking only for "unusual": most fraud is designed to look usual.

Version 3, on all the data

The same design, 3.4 times larger, was then trained on all 25.6 million training payments of the same eight datasets, seven times as much data, in 18.5 minutes on a rented RTX 4090 graphics card. Its average AUROC was 0.833 instead of 0.829. Seven times the data improved the average by only 0.004, and that was the lesson: the model was not short of examples, it was short of information. A payment's amount and hour alone do not show that it is fraud. The model needs to know whether the payment is strange for this customer, and it needs the fraud labels.

Version 4: labels, context and the blend

Version 4 made three additions. The first was context: for every payment, how many payments the customer made in the last hour and day, how this amount compares with the customer's usual amounts, how long they have been a customer, and whether this is their first payment at this merchant, all computed only from what was known at that moment. The second was the supervised model, trained on the labelled fraud and given that context. The third was the blend, which weighs the two models separately for each bank. On the eight datasets version 3 had, the average AUROC went from 0.833 to 0.907 (0.916 over all nine). On the real e-commerce data, the share of fraud caught when 1% of legitimate payments are flagged rose from 0.1% to 35%. The novelty model itself did not improve at all; the whole gain came from the labels and the context.

Version 5: history, entities and supporting models

Version 5 was the largest rebuild. It trained for 2.9 hours on a rented data-centre graphics card (an H200), followed by 40 minutes for the supervised side, the supporting models and the evaluation. The novelty model now reads each customer's previous 32 payments before it judges the next one. The data gained a block of facts about the entities around a payment (the merchant or counterparty, the device, the e-mail domain, the address and the card): how busy each has been recently, how many different customers it has been seen with, and how much confirmed fraud it has had so far. Two more datasets joined, and six kinds of smaller supporting model were added: a specialist for each bank, one that reads the customer's sequence of payments, one for device details, one for the network of connections between customers, merchants and devices, and models for text and for typing and mouse behaviour, which act only when such data comes with a payment. On the nine datasets shared with version 4, the average rose from 0.916 to 0.936. The hardest realistic datasets improved most: Capital One's synthetic card data from 0.765 to 0.838 and the real e-commerce data from 0.858 to 0.940, because the new facts about fraud already confirmed at the same merchant, device or card were exactly the information that had been missing.

Version 5, second refit: fraud labels a week old

One dataset simulates card terminals that are compromised for 28 days at a time. The model counted a terminal's confirmed fraud only once the labels were at least 30 days old, by which time the compromise was over. Counting the fraud a second time, once its labels are one week old (the assumption the dataset's authors use), took that dataset from 0.69 to 0.89 and the real e-commerce data from 0.940 to 0.958. The novelty model did not change; only the supervised side was refitted, which took 30 minutes on the same machine. Whether one week is realistic depends on how fast a bank confirms fraud, which is why the next step made it a setting.

Version 5, third refit: what shipped until version 7

This refit made two changes. It counts a merchant's fraud over the last 7 and 30 days rather than over its whole history, so a merchant compromised this month is not hidden behind years of clean history. And it trains the supervised model with the one-week facts removed from a random 30% of its training rows, so it also scores correctly for a bank whose confirmations take a month. The "labels arrive within" control on the Score tab is that setting. Results: 0.963 average over the nine shared datasets (version 4: 0.916), 0.928 over all eleven, and 0.969 for a payment from a bank the model has never seen. For a bank whose labels take a month, the same model scores 0.935, still above version 4.

Version 6: a model that has read 328 million payments

Version 6 added a different kind of model, with 128 million parameters (the adjustable numbers inside a model; the version 5 model has 3 million). It was trained for four and a half hours on eight data-centre graphics cards to do one thing: read a customer's payments in order and predict the next one, detail by detail. Nobody told it which payments were fraud. It learnt what ordinary sequences of payments look like from 328 million payments, including five extra simulated money-laundering datasets used only for this training. Its reading of a customer's history (a summary, plus how surprised it was by each detail of the new payment) is given to the supervised model as 888 extra facts. On the dataset with thirty years of long card histories, the share of fraud caught when 1% of legitimate payments are flagged went from 86% to 94%. Three other datasets got slightly worse, and the average over all eleven moved only from 0.9277 to 0.9282. Version 6 stays on the site so that this trade can be compared with version 7.

Version 7: the history model for two banks only (what ships)

Version 6's losses had a simple cause: on datasets where the 888 extra facts carry no useful information, they still cost the supervised model accuracy. A control test proved it: the same facts, shuffled so that they carried no information at all, cost those datasets the same amount. Version 7 therefore passes the history model's facts on only for the two datasets where they help, adds a small model trained on fraud labels on top of them, and builds the blend from the models that learnt from fraud. Before these experiments, I built a tool that measures every difference between two versions on the same 8.6 million test payments, with a margin of error. Measured that way, version 7 catches more fraud on average at the same false-alarm rate: 95% instead of 86% on the long-history card dataset (91% instead of 73% for a bank whose fraud labels take a month), and no dataset is worse by more than a few millionths of AUROC. Averages: 0.964 over the nine shared datasets, 0.928 over all eleven, 0.971 for a bank the model has never seen. All of it took one night on two rented graphics cards.

This has one practical consequence. Versions 6 and 7 judge a payment against the customer's earlier payments, so the examples on the Score tab now come with the customer's last eight payments. Sent alone, a payment from a long-standing customer looks to the history model like a new customer's first payment, and version 6 then raised a false alarm on 43 of 150 legitimate examples from the card dataset, where version 5 raised one. With the eight earlier payments attached, versions 6 and 7 both raise one false alarm, and they catch 141 and 142 of the 150 frauds, where version 5 catches 133. Version 7 also handles the case where a payment's own fields say the customer has a past but the past payments are missing: it judges that payment without the history model's facts, a case it was trained for.

5. What did not work

The failures taught as much as the successes, and the repository's training notes record each one. In brief:

  • More data for the novelty model did nothing, three times in a row: seven times as much data changed its average by a few thousandths. Its limit was the information in the fields, not the number of payments.
  • Letting the blend see too much. An early version of the blend was given 81 detailed "surprise" measurements per payment. It scored very well on the data used to tune it and badly on the test data (0.996 and 0.839 on the card dataset), because one of those measurements took extreme values in the newest months and outweighed everything else. The blend now sees only a few scores that stay within a fixed range; the detailed measurements stay on the receipt as explanation.
  • Letting one large dataset define "normal" for everybody. A weighting rule meant to keep the datasets in proportion gave the largest one 85 times the weight of the smallest. Capping that weight at 3 raised the AUROC for an unknown bank from 0.936 to 0.957.
  • Facts that grow with the calendar. "How many customers has this merchant ever had" sounds useful, but on a dataset spanning thirty years it mainly tells the model which decade a payment is from, and the newest months belonged to a decade it had never seen. That dataset lost 0.05 AUROC. With counts over the last 30 days instead of the whole history, it returned to 0.977.
  • A faster, less precise arithmetic mode on the graphics card made the numbers inside the new history-reading part overflow early in training. Full precision was 10% slower and worked.
  • Two training runs stopped at the wrong time, because they watched a quality measure that jumped by up to 0.3 from one pass over the data to the next. The third run watched the training objective itself, which changes smoothly.
  • Memorising a small bank. One dataset has only 21 fraud cases in the half of the data the blend is fitted on, and the blend memorised them. A standard statistical remedy, pulling banks with little evidence towards the shared weights, raised its score on unseen data from 0.915 to 0.953.
  • Two late version 5 ideas. Giving the supervised model the novelty model's summary of the customer's history changed nothing (0.9627 against 0.9634). Training the novelty model for longer on 48 earlier payments instead of 32 was stopped early: the blend gives the novelty model almost no weight, so improving it could not change a verdict.
  • The large history model for every bank (version 6 as it was) made three datasets slightly worse. The shuffled-facts test showed why, and version 7 uses it for two datasets only.
  • Compressing its 888 facts to 64 kept the losses and lost most of the gain.
  • Teaching the history model the fraud labels directly, for 48 minutes, improved its own predictions but not the whole system: it shifted towards the dataset with the most labels.
  • Training the history model longer. Between 10,000 and 60,000 training steps it kept getting better at predicting payments, but the fraud detector built on it did not improve. A second, longer training depended on that test, so it was not run.

6. Why it matters when fraud is confirmed

When a fraudulent payment happens, nobody knows yet. The cardholder notices days or weeks later, and the bank confirms it later still. A fact like "how much fraud has this merchant had" therefore has to be computed from what was known at the time. Otherwise the model learns from information it would not have in real use, and its measured accuracy is too high. FinFraud assumes that labels take 30 days in one block of facts and 7 days in another, and it was trained to work with either. On the Score tab, "labels arrive within" tells it which case applies. The shipped version therefore has two results: 0.964 if a bank confirms fraud within a week, and 0.937 if confirmation takes a month (version 5: 0.963 and 0.935). The history model helps most when labels are late, because it reads payments, not labels: on the long-history card dataset, version 7 still catches 91% of the fraud when labels are a month late, where version 5 catches 73%.

7. How good is it?

Two numbers describe a fraud model. AUROC, explained above, measures how well it ranks fraud above legitimate payments; the shipped version averages 0.964 on the nine shared datasets. The second number is more practical: if a fraud team can afford to review 1% of legitimate payments as false alarms, what share of the fraud gets caught? For the shipped version it is 95% on the large simulated card dataset, 89% on the real European card data, 63% on the real e-commerce data, 79% on the terminal-compromise simulator, 21% on Capital One's synthetic data, whose fraud looks like ordinary shopping, and 72% for a payment from an unknown bank. The three simulators whose fraud follows fixed rules are caught almost completely (99 to 100%), which says nothing about real fraud.

On the datasets where published results allow a fair comparison, FinFraud matches or exceeds them. Compared with what a bank actually runs, it is a demonstration. A real system has signals these public datasets do not contain (device fingerprints, data shared between banks, account histories), is retrained continuously, answers in under 50 milliseconds at thousands of payments a second, and works inside a whole process of human review and regulation. The method here is the method the industry uses; the difference is the realism of the data.

9. Doing it yourself

Everything on this site can be reproduced from the public downloads with the repository's instructions. Two facts are worth knowing before you spend money on a machine:

A faster computer gets you the same model sooner, not a better model. The recipe that produced the shipped version is fixed (the same data, the same model size, the same number of passes over the data). On a slower machine it takes longer and ends with the same model. What does change the result is choosing the smaller recipe made for ordinary computers: a model about a quarter of the size, trained on at most 500,000 to 1.5 million payments per dataset, which finishes in 30 to 60 minutes. For versions 3 and 4 that smaller recipe was almost as good (0.004 and 0.003 lower on average). For version 5 it would be clearly worse, because the part that reads customer histories needs the long histories that only the full data has; it was never trained to completion that way.

Measured times: the small recipe took 32 minutes on two processor cores. The full version 4 recipe takes about two days on a laptop and took 18 minutes on a rented RTX 4090. The full version 5 recipe took 2.9 hours on the H200; on a laptop it would take several days, and on a 4090 about a day (both estimates). Renting graphics cards for version 5 as it shipped cost about $16, the whole version 5 effort including every failed run about $105, and version 7's night $101. Version 6's history model is the one part you would not train at home (four and a half hours on eight data-centre graphics cards), and you do not need to, because it is included in the published versions 6 and 7.

You do not have to train anything. Each version is published ready-made on the repository's Releases page; one command downloads it, and the same app you are looking at then runs on your own computer, with training enabled. The repository's README lists the exact commands.

10. Trying it

  • Pick an example. On the Score tab, the example picker offers test payments, fraud or legitimate, from the six datasets that allow sharing. The receipt shows the model's verdict with the true answer next to it (caught, missed, false alarm or correctly passed), and every version can score the same payment. Where the dataset has customers, the example brings the customer's last eight payments with it, because versions 5 to 7 judge a payment against the customer's own past.
  • Type one in. Fill in whatever you know and leave the rest blank. A blank field means "unknown", not zero, and the model was trained to score payments with missing fields. Choose which bank's normal traffic to judge the payment against, or generic for none of them.
  • Upload a spreadsheet. A CSV file with any of the known columns is scored here, up to 5,000 rows per upload; on your own computer there is no limit. If your file has an is_fraud column, you also get the accuracy numbers for your data.

Nothing you send is stored.

11. What it is not

It is not a product and not advice. It was trained on public, mostly simulated data, and it judges a real payment by its resemblance to the datasets it knows. It shows how such a system is built and how much each idea is worth; it should not decide anything about a real payment.

Who built it: I did (Dean Mostafa), with an Anthropic Claude model helping to write a large part of the code (more of the website than of the training side). The ideas and the decisions about what to build are mine, and the code is open for anyone to read.

12. Glossary

AUROCA number from 0.5 to 1.0: the chance that a randomly chosen fraud is scored higher than a randomly chosen legitimate payment. 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect.
Recall at 1% false positivesThe share of the fraud the model catches when it is set to flag 1% of legitimate payments by mistake.
Supervised model / boosterA model that learnt from labelled examples. Here it is a "gradient-boosted" set of decision trees, a standard and very strong method for data in tables.
Variational autoencoder (VAE)A neural network that learns to compress and rebuild data. What it rebuilds badly is unlike what it learnt from, which makes it a way to measure "unusual" without learning from fraud labels.
BlendThe small final model that weighs the other models' scores, with separate weights for each bank.
GenericThe mode for a payment from a bank the models never trained on: they score it with the bank left blank, a case they were trained for by hiding the bank on part of their training payments.
LabelThe recorded truth: whether a payment was fraud. In real life labels arrive late, which is the subject of section 6.
EpochOne full pass over the training data. The novelty model in versions 5 to 7 was trained for 30 epochs.
GPUA graphics card: the kind of processor that makes training neural networks fast. The H200 and B200 are data-centre cards; the RTX 4090 is a high-end consumer card.
VersionOne trained model you can pick on the Score tab. "v3", "v4" and "v5" are three designs of the data and the model; the three v5 entries share one trained novelty model and differ in the supervised side. v6 and v7 share that novelty model too and add the history model.
History modelA large neural network (a transformer) that learnt, without any fraud labels, to predict a customer's next payment from the ones before it. Versions 6 and 7 give its reading of a customer's history to the supervised model as extra facts.
